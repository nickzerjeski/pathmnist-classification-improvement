\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Standalone Baseline Model}
After establishing the official MedMNIST ResNet-50 benchmark as an external
reference point, the first model trained in this work is a standalone
$224 \times 224$ baseline CNN. This model is not intended to reproduce the
official benchmark architecture. Instead, it provides a lightweight and fully
controlled starting point for experiments on the higher-resolution PathMNIST
images.

The model is a custom small CNN trained from scratch. Its architecture is shown
in Figure~\ref{fig:baseline224-smallcnn-architecture}. It consists of five
$3 \times 3$ convolutional layers. The first two layers map the RGB input from
three channels to 64 feature channels, followed by max pooling. The next two
convolutions increase the representation to 128 channels, again followed by max
pooling. A final convolution maps the representation to 256 channels. Every
convolution is followed by batch normalization and a SiLU activation. The spatial
feature map is then reduced by adaptive average pooling, followed by dropout with
probability $0.2$ and a linear classifier for the nine PathMNIST classes. In
total, the model has 558,921 trainable parameters.

\input{figures/baseline224_smallcnn_architecture}

This smaller architecture is used instead of a ResNet model for pragmatic and
methodological reasons. A ResNet-50 contains far more parameters and is more
expensive to train on $224 \times 224$ inputs, while the small CNN can be trained
and inspected more quickly. It is also easier to analyze layer by layer, which is
important because this work is not only interested in aggregate accuracy, but
also in understanding which visual structures are used for the cancer-related
classes. The model therefore serves as a transparent baseline before adding more
specialized improvement strategies.

\subsection{Training Setup}
The baseline CNN is trained on the $224 \times 224$ PathMNIST dataset for 10
epochs with a batch size of 32. Optimization is performed with AdamW using a
learning rate of $0.001$ and weight decay of $0.0001$. A cosine learning-rate
schedule is used over the training run. To improve robustness, the training
configuration applies histology-specific data augmentation, PathMNIST
normalization, label smoothing of $0.03$, and mixup with $\alpha = 0.05$. The
model is trained with seed 42, and the training run resolves to the Apple
Metal backend.

The validation split is used to select the standalone baseline checkpoint. On
the validation set, the selected model reaches an AUC of $0.9996$, an accuracy of
$0.9873$, and a macro $F_2$ score of $0.9874$. These validation values indicate
that the model can fit the task well, but the final assessment is made on the
held-out test set.

\subsection{Standalone Baseline Results}
On the test set, the standalone $224 \times 224$ CNN reaches an AUC of $0.9935$,
an accuracy of $0.9057$, and a macro $F_2$ score of $0.8850$. The difference
between validation and test performance shows that the model is substantially
less reliable on unseen test examples than the validation metrics alone suggest.
This is particularly important because the target of this work is not only high
global performance, but reliable detection of the cancer-related classes.

For colorectal adenocarcinoma epithelium, the model performs strongly. It
achieves a recall of $0.9886$ and an $F_2$ score of $0.9623$, correctly
classifying 1,219 of 1,233 test samples from this class. In contrast,
cancer-associated stroma remains a major weakness. Its recall is only $0.5202$
and its $F_2$ score is $0.5688$, with only 219 of 421 test samples classified
correctly.

\subsection{Error Analysis}
The confusion matrix in Figure~\ref{fig:baseline224-confusion-matrix} shows that
the standalone CNN does not fail uniformly across all classes. The most important
failure mode is the low recall for cancer-associated stroma. Many true stroma
samples are assigned to visually related or neighboring tissue categories:
78 samples are predicted as smooth muscle, 77 as colorectal adenocarcinoma
epithelium, and 36 as debris. This explains why the overall accuracy can remain
above $0.90$ while the stroma-specific $F_2$ score is still weak.

For colorectal adenocarcinoma epithelium, the model is much more sensitive.
However, some non-adenocarcinoma samples are still predicted as adenocarcinoma,
especially normal colon mucosa and cancer-associated stroma. These errors are
relevant for later model development because they suggest that the model's main
difficulty is not simply distinguishing cancer from all non-cancer tissue.
Instead, the confusion is concentrated around specific tissue types that are
histologically closer to the cancer-related categories.

\subsection{Feature Visualization and Interpretability}
To inspect what the standalone baseline has learned internally, activation maps
from intermediate convolutional layers are visualized for both cancer-related
classes which can be seen in Figure~\ref{fig:baseline224-feature-maps}.
The feature maps indicate that early layers capture local texture and edge-like
structures, while deeper layers produce more spatially selective activations.
For the adenocarcinoma epithelium example, several deeper feature maps appear to
emphasize glandular or boundary-like structures, whereas the stroma example
shows more diffuse textural activations. This suggests that the CNN learns
morphology-related representations, although feature-map inspection alone is
insufficient to establish that the learned features are diagnostically reliable.
\input{figures/baseline224_cancer_feature_maps_figure}

% Grad-CAM highlights the image regions that contribute most strongly to the

To further assess whether the CNN bases its predictions on meaningful tissue
regions, Grad-CAM visualizations were generated for representative samples of
the two cancer-related classes, which can be seen in
Figure~\ref{fig:baseline224-gradcam}.
For the cancer-associated stroma sample, the model activates several distributed
regions across the fibrous tissue structure. The highlighted areas roughly
correspond to regions containing elongated stromal texture and scattered nuclei.
However, the activation is relatively diffuse, which indicates that the decision
may be based more on general texture patterns than on sharply localized
morphological structures which may be one possible explanation for the weak
$F_2$ score of this class. For the colorectal adenocarcinoma epithelium sample,
the Grad-CAM heatmap shows stronger and more localized activations. Several
high-activation regions are located close to dense epithelial structures and
tissue boundaries. Compared to the stroma sample, the model appears to focus
more clearly on spatially constrained tissue regions, suggesting that the
learned representation for adenocarcinoma epithelium may be more localized and
morphologically specific.
\input{figures/baseline224_cancer_gradcam_figure}

\subsection{Baseline Improvement}
As can be seen in the confusion matrix in 
Figure~\ref{fig:baseline224-confusion-matrix}, a substantial number of true
stroma samples are classified as debris, smooth muscle, or colorectal
adenocarcinoma epithelium which is the reason for the weak $F_2$ score. Together
with the fact that the model's Grad-CAM activations for stroma are relatively
diffuse, we can hypothesize that the baseline CNN is too conservative when
predicting stroma.

This observation is further supported by the confidence distribution of true
stroma samples within their predicted classes. As shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:baseline224-stroma-confidence}, true stroma samples that are incorrectly assigned to
debris, smooth muscle, or colorectal adenocarcinoma epithelium often receive
only moderate prediction confidence. This indicates that at least part of the
stroma false negatives occur in uncertain decision regions rather than in cases
where the baseline model is highly confident. Therefore, we can use the
confidence distribution for triggering a second-stage correction step.
\input{figures/baseline224_stroma_confidence_distributions_figure}

The overall confidence distributions for the competing non-stroma classes
provide an additional motivation for this strategy. As shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:baseline224-nonstroma-confidence}, many correctly predicted non-stroma samples,
especially for debris and colorectal adenocarcinoma epithelium, are assigned
with very high confidence. In contrast, the true stroma samples hidden inside
these predicted classes are more frequent in lower-confidence ranges. This
separation suggests that confidence can serve as a useful filtering criterion:
high-confidence predictions can be kept unchanged, while lower-confidence
predictions can be passed to a specialized binary classifier. The separation is
less clear for smooth muscle, where true stroma and non-stroma samples overlap
more strongly in confidence space. Therefore, the smooth-muscle expert may be
more difficult to apply without increasing the number of false-positive stroma
predictions.
